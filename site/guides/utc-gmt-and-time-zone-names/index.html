UTC, GMT and time zone names explained

Every time zone in the world is defined by how far it is ahead of or behind one reference time, called Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC. Knowing how that works makes it much easier to read times written by people in other countries.

UTC and GMT

UTC is the time standard the world's clocks are set by. It is kept by atomic clocks in laboratories around the world and does not change with the seasons.

Greenwich Mean Time, or GMT, began as the average solar time at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London, which was chosen as the world's prime meridian in 1884. Today GMT is also the name of the time zone the United Kingdom, Ireland, Portugal, Iceland and much of West Africa use in winter. For everyday purposes GMT and UTC show the same time.

Offsets

A time zone is written as an offset from UTC. London in winter is UTC+0, New York in winter is UTC−5, and Tokyo is UTC+9. To convert a time, subtract the first offset and add the second: 9 am in New York (UTC−5) is 2 pm UTC, which is 11 pm in Tokyo (UTC+9).

Offsets in use today run from UTC−12 to UTC+14. The furthest ahead are Kiribati's Line Islands, which reach UTC+14, so they are among the first places on Earth to start each new day.

Not every offset is a whole hour

Most time zones are a whole number of hours from UTC, but several are not:

  • India uses UTC+5:30 and Iran uses UTC+3:30.
  • Newfoundland in Canada uses UTC−3:30 in winter.
  • South Australia, including Adelaide, uses UTC+9:30 in winter.
  • Nepal uses UTC+5:45, and New Zealand's Chatham Islands use UTC+12:45 in winter.

Why abbreviations can mislead

Time zone abbreviations are not unique. CST can mean Central Standard Time in North America (UTC−6), China Standard Time (UTC+8) or Cuba Standard Time (UTC−5). IST can mean India Standard Time, Israel Standard Time or Irish Standard Time. When it matters, write the city or the UTC offset instead, for example "10 am Mumbai (UTC+5:30)".

Time zone names in computers

Phones, computers and websites, including this one, use the IANA time zone database. It names each zone after a major city in it, such as Europe/London, America/New_York or Asia/Kolkata, and records every rule change in its history. When a country changes its clocks or its daylight saving rules, the database is updated and devices pick up the change automatically.

Leap seconds

Because the Earth's rotation is slightly irregular, a leap second has occasionally been added to UTC to keep it close to the time given by the sun. In 2022 the world's standards bodies agreed to stop adding leap seconds by 2035.
